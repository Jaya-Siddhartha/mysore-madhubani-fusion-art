Indian Art History · Regional Painting Fusion Project

Fusion of Indian Art:
Mysore Meets Madhubani

This project explores how two very different Indian painting traditions can be brought together in one original artwork. We combined the refined, gold-detailed figures of Mysore painting from Karnataka with the bold patterns and nature motifs of Madhubani painting from Bihar.

Assignment

Regional Painting Fusion Project

Objective

To create and present an original artwork that combines elements of two Indian regional painting traditions.

Understanding the Two Art Styles

Mysore Painting

Origin: Mysore, Karnataka

  • Delicate outlines and finely detailed figures
  • Ornate jewellery, crowns and decorative arches
  • Gold embellishment over raised gesso work
  • Mainly religious and mythological subjects: Hindu gods, goddesses and epic scenes
  • Developed from Vijayanagara painting and was supported by the Wodeyar rulers of Mysore

Madhubani Painting

Origin: Mithila region, Bihar (also practised in Nepal)

  • Bold double outlines and bright, contrasting colours
  • Geometric patterns that fill almost every space
  • Floral motifs, fish, birds, peacocks and decorative borders
  • Religious themes, nature and everyday life
  • Traditionally painted by women on walls and floors for festivals and weddings

Our Fusion Artwork

Saraswati: Mysore Meets Madhubani

Our artwork shows Saraswati, the goddess of knowledge, music and the arts, at the centre. She is surrounded by fish, flowers, birds, geometric patterns and a decorative border.

  • From Mysore painting: Saraswati herself, with her calm face, tall crown (kirita), necklaces, earrings, bangles and waist belt; the veena, prayer beads and palm-leaf book in her four hands; the ornate gold arch with pearls and gems; and the deep red background typical of Mysore paintings.
  • From Madhubani painting: the peacocks, sun and moon, the two patterned fish, the lotus flowers and leafy vines, the dotted background, and the triangle and leaf border, all drawn with bold double outlines and bright colours.
  • Bringing them together: the gold arch is the meeting point: the refined Mysore world sits inside it, and the lively Madhubani world fills the space around it. Shared details join the two halves: the flower garland on Saraswati uses Madhubani colours, and the lotus seat and swan echo the lotuses and water of the outer border, so it reads as one painting.

How We Combined the Styles

ElementMysore InspirationMadhubani Inspiration
Main subjectDetailed central figureDecorative supporting motifs
PatternsOrnamental detailingGeometric and repetitive patterns
ColoursGold and rich traditional coloursBright, contrasting colours
DecorationJewellery and archesFloral motifs and patterned borders

Mysore painting gives the artwork its focus: one graceful, richly decorated figure. Madhubani painting gives it energy: repeated motifs and patterns that fill the space around her. Both traditions are devotional and both love decoration, so they sit together naturally. Mysore adds refinement and gold, and Madhubani adds bold lines and bright colour.

Materials and Method

Materials

  • Drawing paper or canvas
  • Pencils and eraser
  • Coloured pencils, markers or paints
  • Gold-coloured paint or pen for detailing (if used)
  • Black fine-liner for the Madhubani double outlines

The artwork shown on this website was created digitally, as a vector illustration (SVG). The materials above are for a hand-painted version of the same design.

Process

  1. Select the two painting traditions.
  2. Plan the composition.
  3. Draw the central subject.
  4. Add the patterns and decorative motifs.
  5. Colour the artwork.
  6. Review the final composition and document the inspiration sources.

Conclusion

Mysore and Madhubani painting come from different regions and different hands: one grew in royal and temple settings in Karnataka, the other in homes in Mithila. Yet both are devotional, decorative and rich in symbolism. By placing a Mysore-style Saraswati inside a Madhubani world of fish, flowers, birds and borders, our artwork shows their differences (fine detail and gold versus bold outlines and bright colour) and their similarities (devotion and a love of ornament). Combining them into one design is our attempt at creative synthesis: respecting both traditions while making something new.
